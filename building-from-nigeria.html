Fully doable, remotely

Build a working demo

The data behind #1 (Permit Tracker) and #5 (Transit Advisor) is genuinely open to anyone — opentransportdata.swiss's terms don't require registration for file-based data, and GWR's public tier needs no authorization. Nothing about being in Nigeria blocks pulling that data and shipping a working build.

That's also why those two, specifically, are the right starting point — not #2, #3 or #4.

Structurally hard, and it's not about the code

Get paid by a Swiss government body

Foreign bidders get automatic non-discriminatory rights to above-threshold Swiss public contracts only if their home country signed the WTO Government Procurement Agreement. Nigeria hasn't, and isn't in the current accession queue. Below-threshold cantonal procedures are even more informal and default to known local vendors.

A cold "I built this, buy it" pitch to a ministry is a hard sell for structural reasons, not quality ones. A Swiss/EU-incorporated entity or a local partner is what actually opens this door.

Needs Swiss institutional backing first

Get access to sensitive data

Near-real-time GWR data, nursing/workforce records, and grid data all require a demonstrated-purpose authorization request to a Swiss authority. That process is built around institutional legitimacy — a solo foreign applicant with no Swiss affiliation is unlikely to clear it, especially for grid data, which carries critical-infrastructure security scrutiny on top.

This isn't Nigeria-specific — it would apply to a solo builder anywhere without a Swiss/EU institution behind them.

Prototype the pattern — don't try to operate it

Build the unified platform

The shared substrate from the platform brief — identity, interoperability registry, exchange bus — isn't really a product. Its whole job is to be the thing multiple government agencies plug into and trust. No government hands that role to an unaffiliated foreign builder, Nigerian or Swiss. That's a sovereignty problem, not a data-access one, and no amount of good engineering fixes it from outside.

Most of it also already exists and is federally operated — sedex, I14Y and eIAM are live today. The realistic ask was never "replace these," it's "plug a module into them," which still only a Swiss-recognized entity can actually do.

What's genuinely buildable from Nigeria: a demo of the pattern, not the infrastructure. Build #1 and #5 for real on open data, then build a #6-style dashboard on top that reads only what those two publish. That proves the architecture works without asking anyone to trust you with real identity or exchange infrastructure.

Legal obligation follows the data, not you

Handle real Swiss users' data

The revised Swiss Data Protection Act (nDSG) applies to whoever processes personal data affecting people in Switzerland, regardless of where that processor is physically located. Building on open/aggregate data is fine. Adding real sign-ups for Swiss commuters means you've taken on a compliance obligation — know that before you add the first account, not after.

Why Nigeria specifically changes the contract answer

Procurement rights are reciprocal, not universal. Toggle your country of origin.

No automatic bidding rights above threshold

Switzerland grants non-discriminatory bidding rights on above-threshold public contracts only to companies from countries that reciprocate — i.e. signed the WTO Government Procurement Agreement. Nigeria hasn't signed it and isn't in the accession queue, so a Nigerian company has no automatic legal right to bid on a large Swiss federal or cantonal contract. It's still possible — through a Swiss/EU entity, a joint bid with a local partner, or contracts below the threshold — just not automatic.

Bottom line: build the demo, publish it, and take it to the people who publish this data and care about it — opendata.ch's community, cantonal digital-innovation labs, opentransportdata.swiss — before you take it to a procurement office. That's the door that's actually open.

Grounded in WTO Government Procurement Agreement membership rules, the Swiss Federal Act on Public Procurement, and Art. 3 of the revised Federal Act on Data Protection (nDSG). A working analysis, not legal advice.
